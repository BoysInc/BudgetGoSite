Key takeaways

  • Build your budget on take-home pay and on what you actually spent in the last two or three months, not on guesses.
  • Turn yearly costs (gifts, car registration, annual plans) into a monthly amount so they stop wrecking single months.
  • Set savings first, then trim wants until the plan adds up. Your first month is a test, not a pass or fail.

Knowing how to make a budget is less about willpower and more about getting the numbers right once. A budget is a plan for your take-home pay: what comes in, what goes out, and what you keep. This guide walks you through making one from scratch in five steps, with a full monthly example and the math, so you can build your own in about an hour.

Step 1: Find your monthly take-home pay

Start with the money that actually lands in your account after taxes, health insurance and retirement deductions. That's your take-home pay (also called net income). Your salary is a bigger number you never get to spend, so don't budget from it.

  • Paid every two weeks? Multiply one paycheck by 26, then divide by 12. A $1,750 paycheck is $1,750 × 26 ÷ 12 ≈ $3,792 a month.
  • Paid twice a month? Add the two paychecks together.
  • Income changes month to month? Use your lowest month from the last six. Extra money in a good month gets a job when it arrives.

Include regular side income only if you can count on it. In our example, take-home pay is $3,800 a month.

Step 2: List what you actually spend

Open your last two or three months of bank and card statements and sort every expense into three groups. Using real numbers is the step most people skip, and it's why so many first budgets fall apart by week two.

  1. Fixed bills. Same amount every month: rent or mortgage, insurance, loan minimums, phone, subscriptions.
  2. Variable spending. Changes every month: groceries, gas, dining out, shopping, fun. Average the months you looked at.
  3. Non-monthly costs. Bills that show up a few times a year: car registration, gifts, annual subscriptions, car repairs. Add up a year's worth and divide by 12.

Not sure where your money goes? Track your spending for a few weeks first. It makes every other step easier. While you're in your statements, it's also worth a quick subscription audit.

For context, the average U.S. household spent 33.4% of its total spending on housing, 17.0% on transportation and 12.9% on food in 2024, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Your numbers will differ, but those three are usually the biggest lines in any budget.

A positive number is good news, but $395 left over isn't a plan yet. If it stays unassigned, it tends to disappear into everyday spending. If your number is negative, you're spending more than you earn, and Step 4 is where you fix it.

Step 4: Set your targets, savings first

Decide what you want to keep each month before you decide what to cut. Common first goals: a starter emergency fund, extra debt payments, or saving for something specific. The 50/30/20 rule suggests about 20% of take-home pay for savings and debt payoff, which is a useful target even if you can't hit it yet.

In our example, the goal is $500 a month in savings (about 13%). That's $105 more than the $395 left over, so the plan trims two wants:

  • Dining out: $260 → $200 (−$60)
  • Shopping: $180 → $135 (−$45)

$60 + $45 = $105. The new plan: $3,300 of spending + $500 savings = $3,800. Every dollar now has a job.

Cut wants before needs, and cut in small steps. Dropping dining out from $260 to $0 looks great on paper and lasts about nine days. If the numbers still don't work after trimming wants, look at the big fixed bills next: insurance quotes, your phone plan, or a cheaper internet tier.

Step 5: Track your spending and adjust after month one

A budget only works if you can see how you're doing before the month ends. Check in once a week for five minutes: what's left in each category, and is anything running hot?

When a category goes over, move money from another one instead of pretending it didn't happen. If groceries run $40 over, take $40 from fun. If the same category runs over two months in a row, the number was wrong, so raise it and lower something else.

At the end of the first month, compare plan versus reality. Most first budgets need two or three rounds of small changes before they feel right. That's normal, and it's the point.

Which budgeting method should you use?

The five steps above work with any method. The method just decides how detailed your categories are:

Frequently asked questions

How do I make a budget for the first time?

Start with your take-home pay, list what you spent over the last two or three months, subtract, then set a savings target and trim wants until the plan adds up. Expect to adjust it after the first month.

What should a beginner budget include?

Fixed bills, variable spending like groceries and gas, a monthly set-aside for non-monthly costs, savings, and a small buffer of $50 to $100 for things you forgot.

What if my expenses are more than my income?

Cut wants first, then look at the biggest fixed bills (housing, car, insurance, phone). If there's still a gap, it's an income problem as much as a spending one, and even a small side income or raise helps.

How often should I update my budget?

Check in weekly for a few minutes and do a fuller review at the end of each month. Update it any time your income or a big bill changes.
