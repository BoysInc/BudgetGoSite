Spending by Merchant: Why Your Top Merchants Say More Than Your Categories

Categories tell you what you spent on. Merchants tell you who you keep paying, and how often. That's where the habits show up.

Key takeaways

  • Spending by merchant shows who gets your money and how often. Categories only show what kind of thing you bought.
  • For any merchant you visit a lot, the useful math is visits × average spend. Change one of the two and the total moves.
  • Sort your top merchants into five types: fixed, frequent, catch-all, recurring and unclear. Each one needs a different fix.
  • Pick one merchant to change per month, then check the same list next month.

If you want to know where your money really goes, track your spending by merchant, not just by category. A category total like “Dining $390” sounds reasonable. The merchant list behind it, eight delivery orders and fourteen coffees, tells a more useful story. This guide shows how to read your top merchants, the simple math that turns them into savings, and how BudgetGo lines them up for you.

Categories vs merchants: what each one tells you

Categories group spending by type: groceries, transport, shopping. They're great for setting limits and for a quick check against a plan like the 50/30/20 rule. But they blur together very different habits. One big weekly grocery shop and ten top-up trips to the corner store can land on the same total.

Merchants group spending by who you paid. That view answers different questions:

  • How often? Fourteen visits to one coffee shop is a habit. One visit is a treat.
  • How much each time? A rising average order size is easy to miss in a category total.
  • Where's the leak? One merchant can quietly take more than a whole category you watch closely.

You need both. Categories set the plan. Merchants explain why the plan worked or didn't.

A worked example: one month, by merchant

Here's a month of spending for Jordan, sorted by merchant instead of category. Rent is included because it's real money out, even though there's nothing to change there this month.

By category, Jordan's dining total would look like one line. By merchant, two habits stand out:

  • DoorDash: 8 orders × $26.59 = $212.75 a month, or about $2,553 a year.
  • Starbucks: 14 visits × $6.75 = $94.50 a month, or $1,134 a year.

Jordan doesn't have to quit either one. Halving delivery to four orders a month saves about $106 a month, which is roughly $1,277 a year, from changing a single merchant habit.

For context, the average US household spent $4,142 on food away from home in 2025, about $345 a month, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Expenditure Survey. That's an average across all kinds of households, so treat it as a rough reference point, not a target.

The five kinds of merchants on your list

Once you have your top merchants, sort each one into a type. The type tells you what to do next.

1. Fixed merchants

Rent, your mortgage lender, utilities, insurance. They're often your biggest lines, but they rarely change month to month. Leave them alone in your monthly review and revisit them when a contract renews.

2. Frequent merchants

Coffee shops, delivery apps, rideshare, the corner shop. Each payment is small, so they never feel expensive. This is where the visits × average math matters most. Cutting the number of visits is usually easier than cutting the price of each one.

3. Catch-all merchants

Amazon, Walmart, Target and other big stores sell everything from groceries to gadgets. A high total here doesn't tell you much on its own. Open the transactions and ask what you actually bought. Often it's one or two categories, like household restocks or impulse buys, that drive the number.

4. Recurring merchants

Streaming, apps, gym memberships and anything else that charges on a schedule. If several of these appear in your top merchants, it's time for a subscription audit.

5. Unclear merchants

Some card descriptors show a payment processor or parent company instead of the shop you visited. Payment apps and transfers can be just as vague: a transfer to a friend might be rent, dinner or a loan. Give these a quick look so they don't hide a habit.

How to review your top merchants in 10 minutes

  1. Pick a full period. A whole month gives frequent merchants time to show up. One week is too short to spot patterns.
  2. List your top 5–10 merchants. Note the total and the number of visits for each.
  3. Work out the average. Total ÷ visits. Compare it with what you thought you spend there.
  4. Multiply by 12. The yearly number is often what makes a small habit feel real.
  5. Choose one merchant to change. Fewer visits, a lower average, or a cheaper swap. Just one, so it sticks.
  6. Check again next month. Same list, same period length. Did that merchant drop?

This fits neatly into the habit of tracking described in our guide to tracking your spending without a spreadsheet. If you want a sharper reset, a short no-spend challenge on your top frequent merchant works well.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to track spending by merchant or by category?

Use both. Categories are better for setting limits and checking a plan. Merchants are better for finding the specific habits behind a category total, like how often you order delivery.

How many merchants should I review each month?

Your top five to ten is enough. They usually cover most of your flexible spending, and a short list is easier to act on.

Why does one store show up under different names?

Card descriptors vary. A shop might appear under a parent company or payment processor name, or with a location code. Glance at unclear names during your review so a habit isn't split across several lines.

Can I see top merchants without setting a budget?

Yes. BudgetGo's analytics work from your tracked spending, so you can review your top merchants in tracking-only mode or with one active budget.
