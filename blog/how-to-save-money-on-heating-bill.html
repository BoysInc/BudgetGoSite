How to Save Money on Your Heating Bill This Winter (With the Math)

Winter bills don't have to be a surprise. Plan the jump, cut what you can, and keep the rest in your budget.

Key takeaways

  • Use last winter's bills to estimate this winter's. The jump from your autumn bill is what you need to plan for.
  • Start with the free and cheap fixes: a lower thermostat at night and when you're out, closed-off rooms, sealed drafts and a serviced system.
  • Give heating its own number in each winter month's budget, and take the extra from wants, not from savings.

Searches for how to save money on your heating bill climb every November, as the first cold week turns into the first big bill. Most guides give you a list of tips. This one also shows you how to save money on your heating bill with a plan: work out what winter will cost you, cut it where it counts, and fit the rest into your budget with real numbers.

What heating will cost this winter

It depends on how you heat your home. In its October 2026 outlook, the U.S. Energy Information Administration expects average winter spending to fall about 9% for homes heated with natural gas and about 3% for propane, but to rise about 4% for electricity and about 21% for heating oil, compared with last winter (EIA, October 2026).

Those are national averages for November through March. Your own bill depends on your climate, your home and your habits, so your best forecast is the one in your own bill history.

How much is a heating bill per month for you?

Pull up last year's bills for November through March, plus one mild month such as October. The mild month is your baseline: lights, appliances and hot water. Everything above it in the winter months is roughly what heating costs you.

  • Add up the five winter bills. That's your winter total.
  • Subtract five times your baseline. That's your winter heating extra.
  • Adjust for this year. If you've moved, changed tariffs or your provider raised rates, nudge the estimate up or down.

If you don't have last year's bills, ask your provider. Many show 12 months of usage in their app or online account.

How to lower your heating bill in winter: the cheap fixes first

Start with the changes that cost little or nothing. They are also the ones you can do this weekend.

  • Turn the thermostat back at night and when you're out. The U.S. Department of Energy says turning it back 7–10°F for 8 hours a day can cut heating and cooling costs by as much as 10% a year (Energy.gov). A programmable or smart thermostat makes it automatic.
  • Heat the rooms you use. Close doors and vents in spare rooms, and keep the heat where people actually are.
  • Stop the drafts. Draft stoppers under doors and weatherstripping around windows are cheap and keep warm air in.
  • Let the sun help. Open curtains on sunny days and close them at dusk.
  • Service the system. Change the furnace filter, bleed radiators and book a check-up if it's been a while. A system that runs well uses less fuel.
  • Turn down the water heater. Hot water is part of the same bill, and many tanks are set hotter than you need.

Bigger upgrades such as insulation or a new heating system can pay off over years, but they're projects, not quick wins. Price them once you've seen what the cheap fixes do to your bill.

A worked example (with the math)

Say your October bill was $70 and last winter's bills looked like this:

Example winter energy bills and the heating extra above a $70 baseline
MonthBillAbove $70 baseline
November$150$80
December$210$140
January$230$160
February$190$120
March$140$70
Total$920$570
  • Winter total: $920, or $184 a month on average.
  • Heating extra: $920 − (5 × $70) = $570 over the winter.
  • If a thermostat setback trims your heating by up to 10%, that's up to $570 × 10% = about $57 back this winter. Closed-off rooms, sealed drafts and a cleaner filter can add to that.

Now plan for the rest. The biggest month is January, at $160 above normal. If your monthly budget is $3,000, raise your utilities allocation from $70 to about $230 that month and take the extra $160 from wants such as eating out or shopping. If you use the 50/30/20 rule, that's a shift inside the month, not a cut to your savings.

Prefer one steady number? Set aside the winter average of $184 every month from November to March, or ask your provider about a budget billing plan, which spreads your yearly cost into equal payments. Either way, January stops being a shock.

How to save money on heating in an apartment

Renters can't add insulation or replace a furnace, but most of the savings above still work. Use the thermostat setback, draft stoppers and curtains. Removable window film is a renter-friendly way to cut drafts from old windows. If a radiator or window is faulty, report it in writing so it gets fixed rather than costing you every month. And check your lease: if heat is included in your rent, your savings come from comfort, not from the bill.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to leave the heat on all day?

Usually not. Turning the thermostat back while you sleep or are out generally uses less energy than holding one temperature all day, which is why the Department of Energy recommends setbacks. Some heat pumps work best with smaller setbacks, so check your system's guidance.

How much should I budget for heating each month?

Use last winter's bills. Subtract your mild-month baseline to find the heating extra, then adjust for rate changes. In the example above, that meant planning about $184 a month on average, with January as the peak.

What if I can't afford my heating bill?

Call your provider before the bill is due and ask about payment plans or budget billing. In the U.S., the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) helps eligible households with heating costs.
