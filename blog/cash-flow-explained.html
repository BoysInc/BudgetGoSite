Cash Flow, Explained: Money In vs Money Out (With an Example)

One subtraction tells you whether your month is working. Here's how to get it right, with real numbers.

Key takeaways

  • Personal cash flow is money in minus money out over a set period, usually a month.
  • Positive cash flow means you kept some of what you earned. Negative means you spent more than came in, and the gap came from savings or debt.
  • Transfers between your own accounts and credit card payments aren't spending. Count them and your cash flow looks worse than it is.
  • Watch the trend over a few months, not one month on its own.

Personal cash flow is the simplest money check there is: add up the money that came in this month, subtract the money that went out, and look at what's left. That one number tells you whether your month is working. Below is the formula, a worked example, the counting mistakes that throw it off, and what to do when the number comes out negative.

What is personal cash flow?

Cash flow is the movement of money through your accounts over a period of time. For a household, there are only two sides:

  • Money in (inflows): take-home pay, side income, refunds, benefits, interest, and money people pay you back.
  • Money out (outflows): rent or mortgage, bills, groceries, eating out, transport, subscriptions, debt payments, and everything else you spend.

Businesses use cash flow statements to see whether they can pay their bills. Your version answers the same question at a smaller scale: did this month pay for itself?

The cash flow formula (with an example)

The formula is one line:

Net cash flowMoney in − money out = net cash flow

Here's a month for Maya, who takes home two paychecks of $2,100 and does some freelance design on the side.

Maya's money in for the month
Money inAmount
Paychecks (2 × $2,100)$4,200
Freelance project$150
Total in$4,350
Maya's money out for the month
Money outAmount
Rent$1,450
Utilities and phone$240
Groceries$520
Eating out$260
Transport$240
Student loan$310
Subscriptions$45
Shopping and everything else$335
Total out$3,400

Maya's net cash flow is $4,350 − $3,400 = +$950. To see it as a share of income, divide by money in: $950 ÷ $4,350 ≈ 22%. She kept about a fifth of what she earned this month.

That percentage is worth tracking because it scales with you. A $950 surplus means something different on $4,350 than on $10,000, but "kept 22%" compares cleanly from one month to the next.

Positive vs negative cash flow: what it means

Positive cash flow means more came in than went out. The surplus is what funds an emergency fund, extra debt payments or a goal. If you don't direct it anywhere, it tends to sit in checking and slowly get spent.

Negative cash flow means you spent more than you earned. The difference had to come from somewhere: your savings, a credit card balance, or a buy-now-pay-later plan. One negative month isn't a crisis, especially in a month with a big annual bill or the holidays. Several in a row is a signal that your fixed costs or habits have outgrown your income.

For context, Americans as a whole saved 3.8% of their disposable income in September 2026, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis. Budget guidelines like the 50/30/20 rule aim much higher, at 20% for savings and extra debt payments. Your own trend matters more than either number.

Common cash flow mistakes (and how to avoid them)

The math is easy. Counting the right things is where most people go wrong.

Counting transfers as spending

Moving $500 from checking to savings isn't money out. It's still yours. If you count it as spending, Maya's month drops from +$950 to +$450 for no real reason. Leave transfers between your own accounts out of both sides.

Counting credit card spending twice

If you count each card purchase when you make it, don't count the card payment at the end of the month too. That's the same money twice. Pick one: either track the individual purchases (more useful, because you can see what they were) or the payment, not both.

Forgetting irregular bills

Car insurance, annual subscriptions and holiday gifts don't show up every month, so a quiet month can look better than it really is. Spread them out: a $720 insurance bill every six months is $120 a month. Our guide to tracking subscriptions and recurring bills covers how to see these coming.

Judging by a single month

A month with three paychecks, a tax refund or a big vet bill will swing the number. Look at three months side by side before deciding your cash flow has a problem.

Cash flow vs budget: what's the difference?

A budget is a plan: how much you intend to spend, and where. Cash flow is the result: what actually came in and went out. One looks forward, the other looks back.

They work best together. Cash flow tells you whether the month worked. Your budget categories tell you why. If cash flow turns negative, the categories that went over are where to look first. And if you don't want a budget at all, cash flow on its own is still a useful monthly check.

How to improve your cash flow

There are only two levers: more in, or less out. In practice, a few moves do most of the work.

  1. Start with what changed. Compare this month with last month and find the one or two categories that jumped. That's usually where the money went.
  2. Cut recurring costs first. A cancelled $15 subscription saves $180 a year with no ongoing willpower. Run a quick subscription audit.
  3. Set aside for irregular bills. Put the monthly share of annual costs aside so they stop wrecking single months.
  4. Give the surplus a job. Move part of a positive month into savings or extra debt payments as soon as you're paid, before it gets spent.
  5. Look at income too. A raise, a side project or selling things you don't use all count as money in.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate personal cash flow?

Add up all the money that came in during the month, add up all the money that went out, and subtract the second from the first. Leave out transfers between your own accounts, and don't count credit card purchases and the card payment both.

What is a good monthly cash flow?

Any positive number is a good start. Many people aim to keep around 20% of take-home pay for savings and extra debt payments, in line with the 50/30/20 rule. More important than any target is whether your cash flow is steady or improving over several months.

Is negative cash flow always bad?

No. A month with an annual bill, travel or holiday spending can dip below zero and still be fine if you planned for it. Negative cash flow month after month is the warning sign.

Do I need a budget to track cash flow?

No. Cash flow only needs your money in and money out. A budget adds a plan to measure against, which helps explain why a month went the way it did.
