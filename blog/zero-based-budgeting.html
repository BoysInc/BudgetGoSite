Zero-Based Budgeting, Explained (With a Monthly Example)

Income minus every planned dollar equals zero. Here's how to build one, with the math, and how to keep it working all month.

Key takeaways

  • Zero-based budgeting gives every dollar of take-home pay a job, so income minus your plan equals $0.
  • "Zero" doesn't mean spending everything. Savings, extra debt payments and sinking funds are jobs too.
  • When one category runs over, move money from another. Don't add money you don't have.

Zero-based budgeting is a simple idea: before the month starts, you decide where every dollar of your take-home pay will go, until nothing is left unassigned. Below you'll find how it works, a full monthly example with the math, what to do when your income changes from month to month, and how to keep the plan on track once the spending starts.

What is zero-based budgeting?

A zero-based budget is a monthly plan where income minus everything you assign equals zero. Rent, groceries, savings, debt payments, dinners out, next year's car insurance: each gets a dollar amount until the leftover is exactly $0.

The method started in business. Companies use it to rebuild each budget from scratch instead of copying last year's numbers, and it was first introduced to the public in a 1970 Harvard Business Review article by Peter Pyhrr, according to McKinsey. For personal budgets, the idea is the same: every expense earns its place each month, and no money drifts around without a plan.

The formulaTake-home pay − (savings + bills + spending + set-asides) = $0

How to make a zero-based budget in 7 steps

  1. Start with take-home pay. Use what actually lands in your account after taxes and deductions, not your salary.
  2. Pay future you first. Assign savings, retirement contributions you make yourself, and any extra debt payments before anything else.
  3. Add fixed bills and minimums. Rent or mortgage, utilities, phone, insurance and minimum debt payments.
  4. Add variable needs from real numbers. Groceries, gas and transit are hard to guess. Use what you actually spent last month. If you don't know, track your spending for a few weeks first.
  5. Set aside money for non-monthly costs. Annual subscriptions, car repairs, gifts and holidays. Divide each yearly cost by 12 and put that amount in a sinking fund.
  6. Fill in wants and a small buffer. Dining out, hobbies, shopping. A buffer of $50 to $150 covers the things you forgot.
  7. Make it zero. If money is left over, give it a job (usually savings). If you're short, cut wants first, then trim variable needs.

Paid twice a month? Build the plan per paycheck. Match each bill to the paycheck that arrives before its due date, then make each paycheck reach zero on its own.

Say you take home $4,200 a month. Here's one way to assign all of it:

$4,200 − $4,200 = $0. Every dollar has a job, and $850 of it (about 20%) goes to savings, extra debt payments and set-asides. The other $3,350 is what you plan to spend this month.

Notice the sinking-fund line. Most budgets break in the month a $600 car repair or a holiday shows up. Setting aside $200 every month means that money is already waiting when the bill arrives.

What to do when you overspend a category

You will overspend something. That's normal, and it's where zero-based budgeting is most useful. The rule: move money, don't invent it.

If groceries run $60 over, take $60 from dining out or the buffer, and your plan still adds up to zero. If one category keeps running over month after month, the number was wrong. Raise it next month and lower something else. At the end of each month, any leftover can roll into savings or next month's plan.

Zero-based budgeting with irregular income

If you freelance, work shifts or earn commission, plan on your lowest recent month, not your average.

Say your last six months ranged from $3,100 to $4,500. Build your zero-based budget on $3,100: savings, bills and needs first, then wants with what's left. In a month that brings in $4,500, the extra $1,400 gets a job the day it arrives, for example:

  • $600 to a buffer that tops up your next lean month
  • $500 to your emergency fund
  • $300 to extra debt payments

$600 + $500 + $300 = $1,400, so you're back at zero. Once the buffer holds a full month of your baseline, you can start each month living on last month's income, which takes most of the stress out of uneven pay.

Zero-based budgeting vs. 50/30/20 and envelopes

Comparison of zero-based, 50/30/20 and envelope budgeting
MethodHow it worksBest for
Zero-basedEvery dollar gets a specific job until $0 is leftDetail lovers, tight months, paying off debt
50/30/20Three broad buckets: needs, wants, savingsA quick starting point with less upkeep
EnvelopesA fixed amount per category; stop when it's emptyControlling spending in problem categories

They mix well. Many people use 50/30/20 to set the rough shape, then zero-based budgeting to fill in the exact dollars.

Pros and cons

  • Pro: nothing slips through the cracks, and savings happen on purpose instead of with whatever's left.
  • Pro: it shows exactly what you can cut when money is tight.
  • Con: it takes time. Plan on an hour to set up and a few minutes a week to keep current.
  • Con: it only works if your categories match real spending, which means you need to see where your money actually goes.

Frequently asked questions

Does zero-based budgeting mean spending all my money?

No. "Zero" means every dollar is assigned, not spent. Savings, retirement, extra debt payments and sinking funds all count as assigned.

Is zero-based budgeting good for beginners?

Yes, if you're willing to spend a little time on it. It works best once you know roughly what you spend, so track a few weeks of spending first, then build your first plan from real numbers.

Is zero-based budgeting better than the 50/30/20 rule?

Neither is better for everyone. 50/30/20 is quicker to set up; zero-based budgeting gives you more control. If 50/30/20 feels too loose, try zero-based for two or three months and see if it sticks.

Can I do zero-based budgeting without a budgeting app?

Yes. A spreadsheet or notebook works. The hard part is keeping track of what you actually spend, which is where an app that brings in your spending automatically saves the most time.
