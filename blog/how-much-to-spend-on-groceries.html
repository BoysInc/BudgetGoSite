How Much Should You Spend on Groceries? (With the Math)

Averages don't know your household. Your last three months of receipts do.

Key takeaways

  • The best grocery budget starts from what you actually spent over the last three months, not from a national average.
  • Turn the monthly number into a weekly cap, plus one stock-up line, so you can see overspending while it's still small.
  • Keep groceries and eating out as separate categories. Mixing them hides where the money really goes.

How much to spend on groceries per month is one of the most common budgeting questions, and most answers hand you an average for "a family of four". Your household isn't an average. This guide shows you how to find how much to spend on groceries from your own spending, with a full example, a weekly cap and a simple way to keep it on track.

Why grocery budgets drift

Groceries are the biggest flexible bill most people have, and they're paid in small pieces: a quick top-up here, a delivery there. No single trip feels big, so the total creeps up without a clear moment where you chose to spend more.

Prices matter too, though less than it feels like. Food at home prices rose 1.8% over the 12 months to September 2026 and slipped 0.1% in September itself, according to the latest CPI figures reported by Supermarket News. If your bill is rising faster than that, it's usually habits, not the shelf price.

Step 1: Find your real grocery number

Skip the averages and look back. Add up what you spent on groceries in each of the last three months, then divide by three. Three months smooths out a big holiday shop or a quiet month away.

  • Include everything food-at-home: supermarkets, markets, delivery orders and top-up trips.
  • Leave out eating out and takeaway. Those belong in their own category.
  • Count household items separately if you can, such as cleaning supplies and toiletries, so they don't inflate "food".

If you don't have the numbers, that's the real finding. Our guide to tracking spending without a spreadsheet shows how to build that history quickly.

Step 2: Set a monthly grocery budget

Your three-month average is your starting point, not your target. Pick a small, realistic cut, such as 5% to 10%, and see if you can hold it for a month. Big cuts fail because they ignore how you actually eat.

Then check it against your whole budget. If you follow the 50/30/20 rule, groceries sit in "needs", so a lower number here leaves more room for wants and savings.

A worked example (with the math)

Say two adults take home $4,000 a month. Over three months they spent $412, $455 and $438 on groceries.

  • Average: ($412 + $455 + $438) ÷ 3 = $1,305 ÷ 3 = $435
  • Aim for a 10% cut: $435 × 0.90 = $391.50
  • Share of take-home: $391.50 ÷ $4,000 = about 9.8%

Now split the $391.50 so it's easy to follow:

That adds up to $391.50. Your weekly number is what you check on a Sunday evening. If the first two weeks come in at $75 and $90, you know you have $60 less to work with, and there's still time to adjust.

How much to spend on groceries per person

A per-person figure is handy for planning, but it only works from your own base. Divide your monthly number by the people in your home: $391.50 across two adults is about $196 each. Children, teenagers, dietary needs and how often you cook at home all change the real figure, so don't compare yourself with a stranger's list.

If you want an outside benchmark, the US Department of Agriculture publishes monthly food plan costs at four levels, from thrifty to liberal, by age and household size. Check the latest figures on the USDA monthly cost of food reports page rather than trusting a blog's number.

How to save money on groceries without a strict diet

Once you have a number, a few habits keep you under it without feeling deprived:

  • Plan three dinners, not seven. Leave room for leftovers and nights you don't feel like cooking.
  • Shop with a list and a weekly cap. Decide the cap before you walk in.
  • Take your top-ups seriously. A $12 stop three times a week is $144 a month.
  • Watch the swap. If groceries fall and eating out rises by the same amount, you haven't saved anything.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I spend on groceries per month?

Start with your average over the last three months, then aim to trim it by 5% to 10%. A good check is the share of your take-home pay: in the example above, about 9.8%.

Should groceries and eating out be separate categories?

Yes. They behave differently, and one often grows when the other shrinks. Separate lines show you which one is really driving your food spending.

What if my grocery bill keeps going over?

Check the weekly cap first, then your top-up trips. If the number is still too tight after a month, raise it a little and cut elsewhere, rather than leaving a budget you never meet.

Do I need a budget to track groceries?

No. You can track spending with no budget and still see your grocery total. A budget adds a limit you can check against.
